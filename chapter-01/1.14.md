# Count-change time complexity

As the `count-change` is a wrapper, we will need to depict the call of inner recursive procedure `cc`.
`cc` is a procedure of two arguments - the sum of money to count number of changes for, and the number of coins allowed to constitute the change.

The definition of `cc` is at `./count-change.rkt`.

## The call tree for (cc 11 5)

```text
Root: (cc 11 5) -> 4
    Left: (cc 11 4) -> 4
        Left: (cc 11 3)  -> 4
            Left: (cc 11 2) -> 3
                Left: (cc 11 1) -> 1
                    Left: (cc 11 0) 0 -> 0
                    Right: (cc 10 1) -> 1
                        Left: (cc 10 0) -> 0
                        Right: (cc 9 1) -> this will happen in the following manner, giving us "1" on each "right" sub-branch, bubbling up. And it follows the intuition there is only one way to change any (pos.) number of money with 1-cent coins (they are inseparable in the context of this task).
                Right: (cc 6 2) -> 2
                    Left: (cc 6 1) -> 1
                    Right: (cc 1 2) -> 1
                        Left: (cc 1 1) -> 1
                            Left: (cc 1 0) -> 0
                            Right: (cc 0 1) -> 1
                        Right: (cc -4 2) -> 0
            Right: (cc 1 3) -> 1
                Left: (cc 1 2) -> 1
                    Left: (cc 1 1) -> 1 (as above)
                    Right: (cc -4 2) -> 0)
                Right: (cc -9 3) -> 0
        Right: (cc (- 11 25) 4) -> 0
    Right: (cc (- 11 50) 5) -> 0
```

## Time/space complexity analysis

With the (count-change 11) we had many of our branches hitting the base case instantly due to the guards like (< amount 0).

As `n` (sum of money) grows, the smaller fraction of such branches would zero out.
Asymptotically speaking, we may treat this procedure as the one that produces the binary tree of evaluations.

We know that for tree-like recursive processes we estimate the number of steps as the number of leaves in such a tree.

If we omit the fact that the binary wouldn't be exactly balanced, then the number of leaves is est. as $2^n$.

So, $T_{\text{count change}} = O(2^n)$.

As with regular tree-like recursive process, the space complexity will be $\Theta(n)$ as at any point we have to keep in memory only those nodes that are above the current in the call tree.


## Addendum/Errata

The \(O(2^n)\) upper bound is valid, but far too loose. The estimation might be improved immensely.

The key was to look at the expansion of small cases:

```text
Root: (cc 6 1)
    Left: (cc 6 0)
    Right: (cc 5 1)
        Left: (cc 5 0)
        Right: (cc 4 1)
            Left: (cc 4 0)
            Right: (cc 3 1)
                Left: (cc 3 0)
                Right: (cc 2 1)
                    Left: (cc 2 0)
                    Right: (cc 1 1)
                        Left: (cc 0 1)
                        Right: (cc 1 0)
```

Here the strange pattern occurs:
On each level left-node terminates immediately and doesn't produce the nodes on the next level.
And that leads to the fact that there are only two nodes on each level.

Thus, T(n, 1) = 2*n + 1 for all n from 1 up to 6, and by induction, it is true for all natural n.

So, $T(n, 1) = \Theta(n)$

Next, we have to consider the one level above. Let's say, we want to count changes for 11 with two types of coins:

```text
Root: (cc 11 2)
    Left: (c 11 1)  <-- We know what that is already
    Right: (cc 6 2)
        Left: (cc 6 1) <-- We know what that is already
        Right: (cc 1 2)
            Left: (cc 1 1) 
            Right: (cc -4 2) -> 0
```


Here, root and all the right branches represent the subproblems with 2 kinds of coins. The number of such subproblems is the exact number of how many times we can substract 5 (the second kind of coin) from the initial sum until we'd have to stop.

There are at most floor(n/5) + 1 times we can do that (if we count the root, which we should).
We can write this expression as $\Theta(n)$.
Thus, we have $\Theta(n)$ such nodes and subproblems they produce.

Each of such a subproblem is a form of variation of T(x, 1):
- T(n, 1) for root
- T(n-5, 1) for it's right child
- T(n-10) for it's child
- etc.

So, the formula should look like this:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& T(n, 2) = \Theta(n) + \sum_{i=0}^{\Theta(n)} T(n-5 \times i, 1)  \\
&= \Theta(n) + \sum_{i=0}^{\Theta(n)} \Theta(n) \\
&= \Theta(n^2)
\end{aligned}
$$


(or, if we write things out in an elaborated manner, omitting the fact that n/5 might not always be an integer...)
$$
\begin{aligned}
& T(n, 2) = \dfrac{n}{5} + 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{n/5} T(n-5 \times i, 1)  \\
&= \dfrac{n}{5} + 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{n/5} (2 \times n - 10i +1) \\
&= \dfrac{n}{5} + 1 + \dfrac{2 \times n^2}{5} + \dfrac{n}{5} - \sum_{i=0}^{n/5} 10i \\
&= \dfrac{2 \times n^2}{5} + \dfrac{2n}{5} - 10 \times \dfrac{\dfrac{n}{5} \times (\dfrac{n}{5} + 1)}{2} \\
&= \dfrac{n^2}{5} - \dfrac{3n}{5} + 1 = \Theta(n^2)
\end{aligned}
$$


We may follow this further in a simplified manner:

$$
\begin{aligned} 
& T(n, 3) = \dfrac{n}{10} + 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{n/10} T(n - 10*i, 2) \\
&= \dfrac{n}{10} + 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{n/10} \Theta{n^2} \\
&= \Theta{n^3}
\end{aligned}
$$

The same goes for k=4 and k=5, as the recurrence has the same structure: we may substract $\Theta(n)$ times the k-th coin from n, and each of those intermediate results would require us to solve the task for (k-1) coins.

$$T(n,k)= \Theta(n)+\sum_{i=0}^{\Theta(n)} ​T(n−i \times d_k​ , k−1)$$, where $d_k$ is the value of k-th coin.

Thus, the time complexity is:

$$ T_{\text{count change}} = \Theta(n^5) $$

The estimation for space complexity remains the same.
